How the art preference quiz works

Choose the artworks you prefer

CAC Art Lab shows 60 pairs of artworks from a supplied dataset. For each pair, choose A or B based on your own preference. There is no right or wrong answer. The images come from The Metropolitan Museum of Art's Open Access collection.

Compare your choices with AI

After each choice, the quiz shows the supplied AI judges' weighted preference and a supplied feature model's prediction for that pair. These are existing results in the dataset; the site does not call an AI model while you play.

The agreement rates count how often your choice matches each AI preference. A tie is left out of that comparison. Agreement is about similarity of preferences, not a score for artistic quality or a correct answer.

The 462/594 (77.8%) reference uses the supplied full set of 594 pairs. Your round uses a fixed subset of 60 pairs and has its own model/judges rate; the two denominators should not be combined.

Read the feature differences

For each pair, the quiz highlights three measured image features with the largest scaled differences. A higher feature value does not make one artwork more beautiful, and a difference does not explain why an AI model chose it.

Finish and review

The final page compares your full round with the AI judges and the feature model, then revisits up to three pairs where your preference differed most from the judges. Your progress is saved in this browser so you can return to the same round. Every new selection is stored in a central research database before you can continue; there is no browser-only play mode. If saving fails, your pending choice remains in this browser for a safe retry. Choices made in an older browser-only round are not uploaded retroactively. Restart round clears browser progress and creates a new round; previously received online records remain.

Artwork titles, artists, dates, and links to The Met's collection records appear after choosing so this information does not influence the initial comparison. Before choosing, each image has a short visual description for screen readers. Images are from The Metropolitan Museum of Art Open Access collection.
